### I · Beer

## Freedom is a design problem

In six radio talks for the CBC, Beer argued that our institutions were built for a slower world and were failing to keep up with rising complexity. His tool is *variety* — the number of states a system can be in — and Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety: only variety can absorb variety. A regulator that can't match the complexity it faces has two options. It can be redesigned, or it can quietly shrink the variety of the people it governs to make them easier to manage. The second option is how freedom erodes without anyone deciding to remove it.

Beer's answer is to engineer variety deliberately: filter what overwhelms, amplify what's missing, and design viable systems so that each part keeps as much autonomy as the whole can afford.

### II · Meadows

## Structure produces behavior

Meadows gives us the working parts: stocks that accumulate, flows that fill and drain them, reinforcing loops that spiral and balancing loops that hold things steady, and delays that make well-meant interventions overshoot. A system's behavior comes out of that structure, which is why blaming individual actors rarely fixes anything.

Her twelve leverage points rank where to intervene. Adjusting numbers is weakest. Changing a system's goals is stronger, and changing the paradigm the system grows out of is stronger still.

### III · Capra & Luisi

## Life is a network that makes itself

Capra and Luisi supply the biology underneath. Wherever there is life, there are networks. Drawing on Maturana and Varela's theory of *autopoiesis*, they describe a living system as a network that continually produces itself, stable in pattern while its matter is constantly replaced. They extend the same lens from cells to social networks, and then to management and the economy: the shift from seeing a machine to seeing a network.

### IV · Raworth

## The economy, drawn as a living system

Raworth's Doughnut has an inner ring, the social foundation no one should fall below, and an outer ring, the ecological ceiling of planetary boundaries. Between them is a safe and just space for humanity. Of her seven shifts in economic thinking, the fourth, *get savvy with systems*, moves from mechanical equilibrium to dynamic complexity and draws directly on Meadows. Here the earlier three roots surface as an economic picture: an economy embedded in society and the living world, with a new goal.
