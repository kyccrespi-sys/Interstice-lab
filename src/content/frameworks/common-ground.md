### I · The precedent

## A preschool inside a nursing home

In 1991, staff at Providence Mount St. Vincent, a care home in West Seattle, asked for on-site child care. Administrators built a licensed preschool inside the building, between the residents' neighborhoods. It has grown to about 125 children, from six weeks to five years old, sharing the building with residents whose average age is around 92. Children and elders meet in planned activities through the week: music, art, storytelling, lunch, or just visiting. Each side keeps its own professional staff.

The Mount shows the model can work for decades. It also shows what makes it work: two licensed programs, each with its own staff, and shared time that is planned rather than constant.

The research is encouraging but honest about its limits. Reviews of intergenerational programs report better attitudes and confidence in children, and better mental health and quality of life in some older adults, but the benefits are not consistent from study to study. Common Ground is designed to give those benefits the best chance, and to measure them.

### II · Nature

## Light, views and things that grew

In 1984 the biologist E. O. Wilson named *biophilia*, the idea that people have an inborn pull toward living things. The same year, Roger Ulrich published a study in *Science* comparing patients recovering from surgery in matched rooms. Those whose windows looked out on trees went home sooner and needed fewer strong painkillers than those facing a brick wall. Designers now group these lessons into patterns: daylight that changes through the day, views to nature, natural materials, water and the feeling of shelter with a view out.

For children, a large review in *Pediatrics* found the strongest evidence for nature's benefits in physical activity and mental health. Natural elements in early-childhood settings, such as logs, rocks, slopes and plants, are linked to more active play. Play that feels a little risky, like climbing or balancing up high, is linked to more activity and confidence.

Daylight matters for eyesight too. In a randomized trial in Guangzhou, adding 40 minutes of outdoor activity to the school day cut new cases of nearsightedness among six-year-olds over three years. That is why the building's centerpiece is a natural hall under skylights, and why a garage door can lift to turn it into an outdoor yard whenever the weather allows.

### III · Movement

## The same movement, trained two ways

Falls are one of the most common reasons older adults lose their independence. A 2019 Cochrane review of 108 trials found that exercise reduces the rate of falls in older people living in the community by about a quarter, with high-certainty evidence. Programs that mainly train balance and everyday movements work, and programs combining balance, functional and strength exercises probably cut falls by about a third.

Children need strength too. The National Strength and Conditioning Association and an international consensus both hold that properly designed, supervised strength training is safe for young people and builds strength, motor skills and confidence. The World Health Organization recommends muscle- and bone-strengthening activity for children, and varied activity emphasizing balance and strength for older adults.

The overlap is striking. Getting up from a chair, stepping up, balancing, carrying and reaching are what an 85-year-old needs to stay independent and what a five-year-old is learning to master. Common Ground's Strength Studio trains each movement twice, once for each age, and then turns it into a game they can play together.

### IV · Growing

## A garden where knowledge changes hands

The Garden Lab joins indoor hydroponic towers with raised beds outside, at heights an older adult can reach from a chair. Children measure, count seedlings, test water and chart growth: real science with a harvest at the end. Elders who gardened for decades become the teachers. The produce goes to the shared kitchen, so lunch is the experiment's result. The system can borrow from our framework 007, *Thin Air, Deep Roots*, which designs a recirculating hydroponic farm.

### V · The business

## Two licensed services, one building

A shared site usually runs two licensed programs: child care, with its own staff ratios and rules, and an adult day program or assisted living, with its own. Licensing rules differ by state and rarely anticipate each other, so the building needs separate entrances, rooms and records for each, plus planned shared spaces.

The financial case comes from sharing. A 2008 study for Generations United compared shared sites with single-age programs and found significantly lower personnel costs, plus savings on rent because the sites could meet space requirements with less total floor area. The more integrated the operations, the larger the savings: sharing expenses, not just a building, is what lowered costs.

Common Ground can draw on several revenue streams: child care tuition, older-adult program or residence fees, fall-prevention classes that health plans or community members pay for, after-hours gym memberships for families, garden produce and events. Demand appears to be there. Surveys for Generations United found most Americans would want this kind of care for themselves or their families, yet only about one in four knew of such a site nearby.

*This is a design framework, not legal, financial or medical advice. Licensing, staffing and exercise programs should be planned with local regulators, care professionals and physical therapists.*
