### The question

## Fresh greens where the season ends in September

Mountain towns import most of their fresh produce for much of the year. The growing season is short, nights are cold, and the soil is thin. Yet the same mountains offer things a farm could use: strong sun, cold air for cooling, and, in many valleys, hot water underground.

This framework imagines a prototype: a year-round hydroponic farm on a Rocky Mountain valley floor. It is speculative, not an engineering plan, but every part of it rests on published research or a working precedent.

### The precedents

## It has been done, in pieces

In Jackson, Wyoming, Vertical Harvest has run a three-story hydroponic greenhouse since 2016 on about a tenth of an acre. Rotating racks of crops give it roughly three times more growing area than its footprint, and it sells its harvest in a market on site.

In Pagosa Springs, Colorado, the Geothermal Greenhouse Partnership heats a set of growing domes with water from a town-owned geothermal well, and grows food for the community through the winter.

### The constraints

## Light is cheap here. Water and heat are not.

At altitude, the thinner atmosphere lets through more sunlight, including more ultraviolet. That is good for plants under the right glazing. Water is the harder problem. Colorado follows the doctrine of prior appropriation, "first in time, first in right," so a farm needs a legal water source before it needs anything else. And winter nights make heat the biggest running cost.
