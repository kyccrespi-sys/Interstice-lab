### I · The forest

## Many kinds beat one kind

The largest study of its kind, drawing on more than 770,000 forest plots in 44 countries, found that forests with more tree species grow more wood, and that losing species costs productivity at an accelerating rate. On average, a tenth less diversity meant about three percent less growth. The authors pointed to the benefits of turning monocultures into mixed forests.

Mixed forests also tend to hold up better. A 2017 review found them more resistant than monocultures to specialist insects, browsing animals and soil-borne disease, the kinds of shocks that pick on one species at a time. Two details matter for the grid. First, mixing helped most against small, selective disturbances and much less against huge ones. Second, what counted was choosing species that function differently, not simply adding more of them.

### II · The fungi

## A market with no planner

Most land plants live in partnership with mycorrhizal fungi. The plant sends sugars made from sunlight down to the fungus; the fungus sends back minerals such as phosphorus that it gathers from the soil. In 2011, Toby Kiers and colleagues showed in *Science* that both sides keep the trade honest. Roots send more carbon to the fungi that deliver more nutrients, and fungi send more nutrients to the roots that pay more carbon. Control runs both ways, and the partners offering the best exchange are rewarded.

That is a working model of a two-way market: many suppliers, many buyers, and fair terms that no one dictates from above.

A caution belongs here. A famous 1997 study by Suzanne Simard's team found carbon moving between trees through shared fungi, and it grew into the popular idea of a "wood wide web" where trees feed and talk to each other. A 2023 review in *Nature Ecology & Evolution* found that the evidence for those bigger claims is thin and that unsupported statements have spread through the literature. This framework leans on what is well established, the plant–fungus market and the value of diversity, not on trees talking.

### III · The portfolio

## Don't own risks that move together

In 1952, Harry Markowitz showed that the risk of a portfolio depends less on each asset than on how the assets move together. Combine investments whose ups and downs don't line up, and the whole is steadier than any part.

Energy economists, led by Shimon Awerbuch, applied the same math to power plants. A generation mix judged only on average cost can look cheap and still be fragile, because fuel prices and output can swing together. A mix chosen for how its parts vary is more secure. The same holds for weather: wind and sun that peak at different hours and in different seasons smooth each other out.

### IV · The grid

## From plantation to understory

The traditional grid looks like a plantation: a few large power plants, long transmission lines and power flowing one way to customers. A single failure upstream can darken everything below it.

Microgrids change the shape. A microgrid is a group of homes or buildings with its own sources and storage that can run connected to the main grid or cut loose and run alone in an emergency. Researchers at the US national laboratories are now designing networked microgrids that link neighboring ones so they can share power, including community-owned systems in Adjuntas, Puerto Rico, a town hit hard by hurricanes. Linked together, they behave less like a plantation and more like an understory: many local patches, each able to stand alone, all able to trade.
